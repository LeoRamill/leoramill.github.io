> *I feel like a spectator of my own life. Almost as if I were a B-list character.*
>

In the many reviews of this film, "narcissism" is the central theme, the one embodied by the figure of Julie. But is it really narcissism? Or is it simply the word we use when someone refuses to stay put in the place we assigned them? Because the accusation of narcissism often says more about the person making it than about the person it's aimed at.

Julie is a girl who doesn't know what to do with her life. She starts medicine, moves on to psychology, then to photography, and in the meantime she works in a bookshop: not out of inconstancy, but because every door she opens shows her two more she hasn't opened yet. And every choice, in order to exist, has to kill all the others… that's the price nobody tells you about beforehand.

She throws herself into one field after another until she meets Aksel, a man much older than her, a comic book artist known across Norway, whose pages portray the vices of human beings, and above all their sexual instincts.

Through him, Julie discovers the world of writing: a more scientific kind of writing, a more psychological one, about female sexual taboos, asking herself how far the female idea of being completely dominated by a man was a contradiction of the feminist ideals she carries forward. And it is maybe the bravest gesture in the whole film: putting down in black and white the thing that contradicts you, instead of hiding it to stay consistent with the image other people have of you.

It's not by chance that the film is built like a book: a prologue, twelve chapters, an epilogue. A life told with the table of contents of a novel, while the main character isn't at all sure she's the one writing it. And that's exactly what the line at the beginning says: feeling like a spectator, feeling like a B-list character in your own story. So it isn't narcissism. It's the opposite: it's the terror of not being the **subject** of your own life.

There's that scene where the world stops and she runs across Oslo to reach the other one. It's the most honest way of showing what it feels like when you finally choose: for a moment it seems everything else can stay on pause and wait for you. But time doesn't really stop. It starts again, and it brings the bill.

And then there's Aksel, the exact opposite: someone who always knew what he wanted, who built something, who stayed consistent all the way through. The film could have used him as the moral, as the example to follow, and instead it saves the hardest lesson for him: consistency doesn't shelter you from anything. You can have chosen well every single time and still find yourself facing a bill you didn't choose. Maybe that's the point: you don't choose in order to be safe. You choose because it's the only way of really being there.

So who is the worst person in the world? Nobody in this film really is. "The worst person in the world" is just the name we give ourselves at three in the morning, after hurting someone without meaning to, when the only other option would have been hurting ourselves.

Without giving too much of the film away, what the pragmatic figure of Julie can pass on is that life must never be lived passively, and even when it can go badly, even when it means risking everything and suffering a lot, you have to stay true to yourself so you won't end up regretting your own choices. Because the wrong choices hurt, but they pass. The ones you never made don't: they stay there, in a closed room, and every now and then they knock.
