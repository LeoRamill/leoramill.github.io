Osmosis, by definition and according to Treccani, is a phenomenon of diffusion between two miscible liquids through a separating membrane, which can be caused by thermal (thermoosmosis), electrical (electroosmosis) or, in the most common case, by a difference in concentration between two solutions of equal components or between a solution and its solvent. Depicting it in a more social context, it is a mutual attraction between two people born of strongly shared innate elements that drive the two individuals to what we can define as love; or rather, this is the central theme, as well as the paradox, of this TV series, Osmosis.

Without going into detail, Osmosis tells the story of an algorithm capable of identifying one's "soul mate" with mathematical precision by decoding the electrical impulses generated by our neurons. The algorithm, whose explicit functioning remains a "black box," manifests itself through a Generative and Emotional Artificial Intelligence called Martin. This digital entity was created and designed by neuroscientist Esther Vanhove, a complex figure who seeks to rationalize the irrational, transforming feelings into binary code.

For a 2019 production, the series proves prophetic, bringing to the screen cutting-edge themes that, projected into the present day, appear relevant: the use of drugs or nanorobots without real long-term certification, cyber attacks to collect sensitive data, dating dictated no longer by instinct but by scrolling through an algorithm, bioethics, and (the real hype of the moment) the pervasive use of artificial intelligence in neuroscience. Can an algorithm bring good to society despite having access to our hormonal states, blood pressure, and knowing exactly how much endorphin our brain produces at any given moment?

Certainly, finding a "latent space" through the processing of enormous amounts of data (a process that guarantees diagnostic accuracy superior to that of humans) explains how we can prevent disease in a timely manner or understand our own emotions. However, the question arises: how risky is it to have a powerful tool (in jargon, a model) that can "read" and "interpret" the mind, thereby obtaining the terrifying possibility of manipulating it? If AI knows our desires before we even formulate them, free will becomes a statistical illusion; it becomes that confidence interval that is not so 'confident' after all.

If biological osmosis requires a semi-permeable membrane to function, Osmosis shows us what happens when that membrane, the boundary between our private identity and the cloud, between the mystery of falling in love and the certainty of calculation, is torn apart and dissected. Accepting the rules and free will dictated by Martin means accepting to become transparent, readable, and (frighteningly) programmable. Perhaps true love, "human" love, lies precisely in the uncertainty of error, in that margin of risk that no sampled electrical impulse can ever replicate.
